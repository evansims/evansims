---
title: Don't Defend. Explain.
topic: DevRel
updated: 2026-04-29
---

Most of the people reading aren't the one writing.

Maintaining open source means answering unhappy developers in a place anyone can read. I did that across more than 45 libraries at Auth0, and again on OpenFGA.

Write for the audience. The person who filed the angry issue has usually made up their mind. The people who find the thread later haven't, and they're deciding what it would be like to depend on you. Say someone opens an issue titled "this SDK is broken." A reply that corrects their tone wins the exchange and loses everyone reading it. A reply that reproduces the bug, names the cause, and says when the fix ships does the opposite.

Explain what happened without defending it. The same facts read as bitter or as transparent depending on the framing, so take more of the blame than feels fair. Extra ownership costs you little. Looking defensive in public costs a lot.

Sound like a person. Skip the jokes while someone's upset, and skip the corporate phrasing always. If you don't have the answer yet, say that, and say how you're going to get it. People can spot a stall.

Keep the thread open. Locking replies changes the question from "is this person right?" to "can this team handle being challenged?", and that's a worse question to be answering.

On technical complaints, be detailed and calm, and check what the complaint is about. An argument over implementation details is often a question about trust.

If you apologize, name the problem, the fix, and what stops it from happening again. Only do it when you mean it.

Some people won't come around, and that's fine. You're writing to be someone people want to deal with the next time something breaks.

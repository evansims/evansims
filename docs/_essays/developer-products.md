---
title: Developer Products Are Tried, Not Sold
topic: Product
updated: 2026-04-29
---

If they can't try it, they won't trust it.

A developer product gets about an hour. In that hour a curious developer signs up, gets something running, and decides whether it deserves more of their time, without talking to anyone. Pricing, marketing, and sales all come after that hour, and none of them can rescue a bad one.

So walk through the hour the way they will.

First, they have to be able to start alone. Developers want to find out whether something works before they talk to a person. Give them self-serve signup, a free tier that does real work, and an upgrade path they can read without a call. Some software does need contracts and hand provisioning. Ask the developers who went through that, and most would have chosen to start on their own.

Then the docs have to answer their question. They arrived with a specific problem, so the measure is how fast they find the answer to *that*. A quick start should reach a working line of code in five minutes. After that the docs serve as a reference when you know what you're looking for and as a guide when you don't. Introduce one idea at a time, in plain words. Experts won't mind.

When they get stuck, a developer should answer. People can tell within a message or two whether the person replying understands the problem. Cut the relays between whoever has the problem and whoever can fix it. That's the reason developer advocates and customer success engineers exist.

At some point they'll want to see inside. OpenFGA is the example I know best. It's an authorization system, which means teams put it in the path of every permission check. Grafana Labs, Docker, and Canonical did, and they could read every line first. Nobody had to take our word for how it worked. You don't have to open everything. Open what people need to see before they'll depend on you.

The hour ends and the long game starts. You probably don't need your own branded community yet. Go where your developers already spend time and be useful there before you ask for anything. It pays back over years.

If you're deciding what to fix next, sit a developer who has never seen your product in front of it and watch the hour.
